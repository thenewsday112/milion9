Jusqu’au 27 août 2027, Train World, le musée ferroviaire installé en gare de Schaerbeek, tout près de Bruxelles, propose une exposition temporaire sur le lien unissant mode et chemin de fer. Un évènement qui se déroule dans le cadre des 100 ans de la SNCB.

Arrivé dans la belle salle des guichets de l’ancienne gare de Schaerbeek, le visiteur découvre six créations contemporaines imaginées par de jeunes diplômés du College of Art and Design Brussels (CAD), une école supérieure bruxelloise de design. Ces jeunes talents ont été invités par la Société nationale des chemins de fer belges à créer les tenues des voyageurs qui empruntent ses trains. Dans le cadre du centenaire de la SNCB, célébré tout au long de l’année à travers une multitude d’évènements, Train World, le musée de la compagnie ferroviaire belge, propose aux visiteurs, depuis le 18 septembre, « Fashion & Trains. La mode au fil du rail, depuis 1835 ». La scénographie de cette exposition temporaire montre comment l’avènement du chemin de fer et son évolution technique ont influé sur la mode à travers la tenue des voyageurs, mais également celle des agents de la SNCB. Tout au long de la visite, des affiches, des objets et de nombreuses photos constituent une véritable « esthétique » de l’usage du train.

Le premier hall s’intéresse à l’époque de la naissance du chemin de fer belge en 1835 et à son rapide développement jusqu’au début du XXe siècle. Le rail devient alors un symbole ultime de la révolution industrielle et de la modernité triomphante. Tout au long de la deuxième partie du XIXe siècle, les tenues de voyageurs évoluent.

Dans le deuxième hall, c’est la mode de l’entre-deux-guerres qui assure le spectacle. Les tenues diffèrent parfois entre lignes locales, utilisées par les habitants de villages, et trains plus rapides reliant les villes, ou entre les déplacements professionnels et ceux dédiés aux loisirs. Cette dernière dichotomie est explorée dans le troisième hall. La démocratisation des voyages d’agrément influence également l’évolution des vêtements. Dans le quatrième hall, on monte à bord des trains de luxe internationaux et des luxueuses voitures de la famille royale belge de 1901 et 1939.

Robe d’été portée par la reine Fabiola en 1969. © Train World – SNCB

Tout au long du parcours d’exposition, l’évolution des uniformes des agents de la SNCB est mise en lumière, de la tenue d’apparat du chef de gare aux uniformes contemporains, en passant par les premiers uniformes féminins.

En admirant ces différents modèles, le visiteur se rappellera cette sentence définitive de la grande Coco Chanel : « La mode se démode, le style jamais ».

Llangollen Railway plans Remembrance Sunday Poppy Drop and free travel for veterans

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Llangollen and Corwen Railway will mark Remembrance Sunday on 8 November 2026 with a special Poppy Drop led by two Royal Navy veterans, the temporary renaming of steam locomotive No. 3802 and free afternoon travel for Armed Forces personnel, veterans and cadets.

Mandy Towers lays the wreath on behalf of Llangollen and Corwen Railway

The Llangollen and Corwen Railway has announced a programme of commemorations for Remembrance Sunday, including two symbolic journeys through the Dee Valley, station tributes, wreath-laying ceremonies and a railway-wide act of remembrance.

Royal Navy veteran Garry Smith MBE and former Women’s Royal Naval Service (WRNS) member Ruth Smith will lead a special Poppy Drop, carrying poppies on separate train journeys between Llangollen and Corwen.

For the occasion, Great Western Railway heavy freight steam locomotive No. 3802 will be temporarily renamed ‘The Remembrance’ for the day.

The railway will also offer free travel on its afternoon services to serving Armed Forces personnel, veterans and members of recognised cadet organisations.

Remembrance Sunday services at Llangollen and Corwen

The railway’s usual 10am departure from Llangollen will be cancelled to allow staff and volunteers to attend Remembrance Sunday services and parades in their own communities.

At 11am, all activity at Llangollen Station will stop for the national Two-Minute Silence, observed by staff, volunteers and visitors.

Garry Smith MBE and Ruth Smith will represent the railway at the Remembrance Service at Llangollen War Memorial on Centenary Square, laying a wreath on its behalf.

At Corwen, Wayne Ronneback and the Corwen Station Team will represent the railway at the town’s Remembrance Service.

Mandy Towers was joined by Llangollen Railway Trust Manager David Hennigan with volunteers Mandy Towers, Glyn Jones and Mike Jackson The GWR Heavy Freight 3802, will be renamed ‘The Remembrance’ for the day. Mandy Towers lays the wreath on behalf of Llangollen and Corwen Railway Garry Smith MBE and his wife Ruth Smith will be leading Remembrance Day commerations in Llangollen.

Veterans to lead special Poppy Drop through the Dee Valley

Following the morning commemorations, Garry Smith MBE will travel on the footplate of the 12.20pm service from Llangollen to Corwen, releasing poppies on the approaches to stations along the route.

At Corwen, Ruth Smith will take over the tribute, travelling on the footplate of the 1.45pm return service to Llangollen and continuing the Poppy Drop.

The railway says the tribute will be planned and carried out in accordance with its operational and safety requirements.

Garry Smith MBE, a Royal Navy veteran, said:

“Remembrance Sunday is a deeply important occasion for all of us who have served in the Armed Forces. It is a time to remember our friends, our comrades and the generations of men and women who made the ultimate sacrifice.

“To have the honour of laying a wreath at Llangollen and then travelling along our beautiful railway, releasing poppies in remembrance, will be incredibly moving.

“Every poppy represents a life, a sacrifice and a family whose world was changed forever. As we travel through the Dee Valley, we will be remembering those who never came home.

“I am immensely proud to be part of this tribute and to represent the Llangollen and Corwen Railway.”

Ruth Smith, a former member of the Women’s Royal Naval Service, said:

“Remembrance is about more than a ceremony. It is about ensuring that the sacrifices made by previous generations are never forgotten.

“There is something especially poignant about Garry taking the poppies from Llangollen to Corwen and me bringing the tribute back along the line.

“Two journeys, travelling through the communities that our Railway serves, united by one message of remembrance.

“As a former Wren, it is a real privilege to take part in this special occasion. I hope everyone who sees the trains passing will take a moment to reflect on those who served, those who suffered and those who gave their lives for our freedom.”

GWR locomotive No. 3802 to carry ‘The Remembrance’ name

The railway’s Great Western Railway heavy freight steam locomotive No. 3802 will be temporarily renamed ‘The Remembrance’ for Remembrance Sunday.

The name will be carried for the day as a tribute to the service and sacrifice of generations of Armed Forces personnel.

Remembrance displays at Glyndyfrdwy and along the railway

Volunteers at Glyndyfrdwy Railway Station have prepared a Remembrance display featuring two silhouettes representing members of the Armed Forces, accompanied by flags.

Flags and poppies will also be displayed at every station along the Llangollen and Corwen Railway.

The railway hopes these displays will encourage passengers, visitors and local residents to reflect on the sacrifices made by servicemen and women.

Free train travel for Armed Forces personnel, veterans and cadets

The railway will offer free travel on all its Remembrance Sunday afternoon services to serving Armed Forces personnel, veterans and members of recognised cadet organisations.

The offer includes personnel serving in the Royal Navy, British Army and Royal Air Force, former service personnel, and members of the Sea Cadets, Army Cadets and RAF Air Cadets, including the Air Training Corps.

Those wishing to travel free of charge must present appropriate proof of service or cadet membership at one of the railway’s ticket offices.

Accepted identification includes a military identification card, Veterans Railcard, Veterans ID Card or recognised cadet identification.

The complimentary travel offer is intended as a gesture of thanks to serving personnel, veterans and young people involved in cadet organisations.

Remembrance Sunday train timetable – 8 November 2026

The railway has confirmed the following afternoon services:

Llangollen to Corwen:

  • 12.20pm
  • 3.15pm

Corwen to Llangollen:

  • 1.45pm
  • 4.20pm

There will be no 10am departure from Llangollen, allowing railway staff and volunteers to attend local Remembrance Sunday commemorations.

The commemorations will bring together railway staff, volunteers, veterans, cadets and the communities of Llangollen and Corwen.

Through the wreath-laying ceremonies, Two-Minute Silence, special station displays, the temporary renaming of locomotive No. 3802 and the Poppy Drop journeys led by Garry and Ruth Smith, the railway aims to provide opportunities for remembrance along the heritage line.

The Llangollen and Corwen Railway hopes passengers, visitors and local residents will join in remembering those who made the ultimate sacrifice.

100 years of Mail Rail to be celebrated with programme of events

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Postal Museum will celebrate 100 years of Mail Rail next year with a new exhibition.

Credit: The Postal Museum

The London-based museum, which opened in 2017, will launch Powering the Post, it’s new exhibition focused on stories of invention, engineer and experimentation behind the post, including lessons learned from the ideas that didn’t deliver, in Mail Rail’s centenary year.

In 1927, the General Post Office opened the world’s first driverless, electric railway, now known as Mail Rail. At its peak, the service carried four million letters a day beneath London’s streets.

The network would go on to connect eight subterranean stations, from Paddington to Whitechapel.

The railway closed in 2003, but The Postal Museum allows visitors to ride through the network’s underground tunnels.

The museum has announced plans to celebrate 100 years of Mail Rail with a programme of events, lead by the new temporary exhibition, opening on 29 May 2027.

The exhibition will focus on stories of invention and experimentation behind the post – including lessons to be learned from the ideas that didn’t deliver, like Rocket Mail, a failed attempt at delivering letters by missile.

Co-produced with postal workers and local residents from communities local to Mail Rail’s Mount Pleasant station, the exhibition will feature interactive elements which curators hope will inspire the next generation of problem solvers.

Chris Taft, Head of Collections, The Postal Museum, said, “In 1927, an underground fleet of driverless electric trains provided a pioneering solution to a city whose streets were quickly filling with traffic. In Mail Rail’s centenary year, Powering the Post will celebrate the inventive engineers whose spirit of experimentation has helped keep us all connected.”

Further details of the Mail Rail 100 programme will be announced in 2027.

From the 1983 Archives: The North Express – C. Hamilton-Ellis Recalls a Forgotten Railway

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In this archive article, C. Hamilton-Ellis recalls the Midland & South Western Junction Railway and its North Express service. Drawing on his own memories of the railway, he describes its history, locomotives and the journey through the Wiltshire countryside, accompanied by his railway painting.

The North Express and its origins

SUCH a title as the North Express might mean to us, nowadays, anything from a train on its way from Inverness to Wick, to another heading for the Baltic. But this North Express was to be seen at the head of Southampton Water, and on Salisbury Plain, and threading the Cotswolds. So to its origins.

There were several thorns in the side of the Great Western Railway, distinct from the London & South Western, which became rather a sort of sciatica. A true thorn was the Somerset & Dorset, which the dastardly Midland Railway and the unspeakable South Western had jointly snatched in the 1870s. Another was the Midland & South Western Junction Railway. In spite of its name, this was sturdily independent. It linked the Midlands with the Deep South, with the benevolent support of Paddington’s two old enemies, adding insult to injury by getting running powers over stretches of the Great Western.

The Junction Railway’s beginnings

Beginnings of the “Junction”, which so improbably extended from Andover to Andoversford, about sixty miles northward, had been modest and penurious. The Swindon, Marlborough & Andover Railway reached Marlborough in 1881, to push on to Grafton and ultimately to Red Posts Junction, a little west of Andover on the South Western main line. The Swindon & Cheltenham Extension Railway reached Cirencester from Swindon (GW station) in 1883. Desperate, with no apparent friends, the two companies amalgamated next year as the Midland & South Western Junction Railway. Through traffic, crossing over the Great Western at Rushey Platt, began in 1891.

A Receiver was in possession, but Midland, and LSWR, now had their eyes on this second potential dig at the GWR. The South Western released its promising Chief Storekeeper, (Sir) Sam Fay, to be the new General Manager of the MSWJR in 1892. Himself Southampton-born, he moved-in-fast and fairly licked the staggering outfit into shape and solvency. He got running powers, willingly granted, over the South Western from Andover Junction to Southampton Terminus. There were General Offices in an agreeable Victorian villa overlooking Swindon Town Station, run by Old Swindon. In 1899, with mission accomplished, Fay went back to Waterloo as Superintendent of the Line, before going to the toiling Great Central as General Manager in 1902.

The North Express service

For the first time, with the northern extremity completed, the “Junction” prospered. Under Fay, in 1893, came the forerunner of the North Express, with through carriages from Southampton to Sheffield, and a corresponding South Express. The company had then one express engine, a modest 4-4-0 by Dübs & Company. By 1914 at latest, starting early in the afternoon, it was quicker to ride from Waterloo to Cheltenham than from Paddington, thanks to a smart connection at Andover Junction. One recalls arriving at the latter, from London, and seeing the red North Express waiting on the left-hand curve from Andover Town, having left Southampton at 1.45 p.m. It went on to call at Swindon Town, then without a stop to Cheltenham, 35¼ miles, over the Cotswolds, in 54 minutes.

Memories of the railway

That is the train I have painted, with fond memories from long ago. My picture (reproduced overleaf) shows it, in 1915, coming down through Burderop Woods, above the head of Coate Water just south of Swindon, in the heart of Richard Jefferies’ beloved country, the scene of his best books. Almost continuously the line traversed country of singular beauty: along the eastern flanks of Salisbury Plain, through the Marlborough Downs and so by Chiseldon down to Swindon Town. Then came Cirencester, a delightful town to this day save that now its nearest railway station has to be Kemble Junction on the old Great Western line north to Gloucester.

At Cirencester, too, the company had its locomotive shops, and the handsome through station called Watermoor. (The Great Western had but a picturesque terminus, haunt of fussy rail-motors, on the other side of the town). Alas, poor Watermoor! Last time I saw it, twenty years ago, the buildings were deserted; vandalised and mucked-up by young yobos, such as occur even in such a nice borough as “Ciren”.

The locomotives

Going over the Cotswolds entailed marked contrasts, from the windswept plateau by Foss Cross to the delightful passage of Chedworth, and north of its double-tracked tunnel, a lovely descent to join the reluctant Great Western line out to Kingham. So, by running powers, the trains reached the welcoming arms of the Midland Railway.

Midland & South Western Junction trains scarcely resembled those of its mighty allies, save in the beautiful Midland crimson for engines and carriages. The engine tenders proudly bore the company’s initials in gold, in an extremely florid sort of italic script, all curved and scrolled, possibly inspired by that on the splashers of London & South Western express engines during the Adams period. The locomotive in my picture was one of a pair built in 1914, the last of James Tyrrell’s standard express engines. These two, Nos. 4 and 31, had each two domes, that on the second ring containing top-feed apparatus and being surmounted by a pair of Ross safety-valves, highly polished. This made the engines very exotic in the eyes of the small boy who once was I. There was virtue in the carriages, too. All too rarely for that time, all had electric lighting, even the horseboxes. The lavatory tanks were partly raised above the camber of the roofs, rather suggesting Holland.

The end of the railway

All gone, and for a long time now! Under the Railways Act 1921 this railway passed into the Great Western net, though not without a rearguard action lasting into the summer of 1923, the Government’s deadline. The Great Western gradually ran it down to rather a shadow of its old self. Yet it still served faithfully through the second world war, as well as the first conflict. But, as in other parts of the West Country, Paddington was to win in the end.

C. Hamilton-Ellis

NS&T 17 publication award

This S scale freight motor is already my all-time favourite construction project…

This week’s mail included a pleasant surprise: An award from the National Association of S Gaugers (NASG) – recognizing my feature in Railroad Model Craftsman magazine about building Niagara St. Catharines & Toronto Railway freight motor 17.

The NASG is a class act, and creating awards like this can only encourage hobbyists to share their love of 1:64 modelling with the wider world.

Those who enjoy building in niche scale/gauge/prototype combinations often end up talking in an echo chamber filled with like-minded modellers. What’s more, one of the frequent topics is, “How to we attract more hobbyists to our favourite combo?”

This award is a good example of what can be done. It’s specifically designed to recognize modellers who have written an article about S scale for a non-S scale publication. What a great way to expose others to what can be done in 1:64.

Thank you to everyone at the NASG who was involved in this. I’m grateful for the recognition: It means a lot.

I’m also grateful to Otto and the team at RMC for being open to running features about niche combinations: If you have an idea for an article, I encourage you to get in touch with RMC to get it published.

15 years of Port Rowan

📅 Aug 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

My calendar app reminded me that on this day in August, 2011, I embarked on what would be my most successful layout-building project.

A short freight leaves St. Williams, bound for Port Rowan.

That layout was Port Rowan in 1:64 – an S scale interpretation of a CNR branch line in its final years.

On paper, the layout was simple: It filled an odd-shaped space roughly 14 by 35 feet, but featured just two towns (plus staging) and a grand total of eight turnouts. The layout supported one train at a time operation, with enough work to keep a two-person crew entertained for an hour or so. It’s the very definition of what I came to call an Achievable Layout.

But there was a lot more to Port Rowan than that description suggests.

I have hovered around the prototype modelling sphere for decades and have built (well, started) a number of prototype-based layouts. But Port Rowan marked the first time I had built a layout that looked like a model of a railway, rather than a model railway. By exercising restraint during track planning, I ended up with plenty of room to present the railway in context with its surroundings.

I loved that about it.

In a scene that would never have happened on the prototype, two trains meet at Port Rowan. Note how scenery dominates the scene: I loved how I was able to present the railway as part of a larger world.

Port Rowan’s appearance encouraged me to put the railway in context operationally, too. I embraced what I call “finescale operations”, with prototypically-designed paperwork, a working telegraph system, turnouts controlled by switch stands, and other features. It was very satisfying.

As I reflect on what made it such a success – for me, anyway – I’m considering what lessons from Port Rowan I can apply to my current layouts. Overall, I’m pleased with GWR Bydemill and CNR Pine Street. But I think some improvements are in order for my main layout – based on the Niagara St. Catharines & Toronto Railway.

The NS&T is the project that is closest to my heart, and as a consequence it’s the subject on which I have placed the greatest demands. As I look at it today, I realize the layout I’m building is not measuring up. Changes are in order, and Port Rowan provides me with important insights into what those should be.
